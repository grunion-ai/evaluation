The output is one title written as a full sentence: a deck title, a slide title or a section heading. Decide whether a strict editor would keep it as written.

FAIL when any one of these is true. Name the first that applies.

1. False agency. An abstraction, a list of things or a document does a human act of judgment: it decides, sets, settles, determines, drives, dictates, tests, narrows or follows from something. People and organisations decide; questions, reasons, contracts, histories and pilots do not. A concrete subject with a literal verb is fine: a product comes with a suite, a tool runs on a server, a team owns a tool, a feature changes how a team reads.
2. Length. More than 12 words.
3. Advice in place of a point. The sentence tells the reader what they should do, or hangs on "should", "must" or "need to".
4. Loose second person. The sentence leans on "you" or "your" as its subject or its frame ("Your choice", "Your reason", "when you plan it", "a server you host"). "your team" or "your company" as an object inside an otherwise plain statement is acceptable.
5. Diction that does not hold up literally. A figure of speech or a verb that makes no mechanical sense when read word for word ("a cost pushes it out", "the move goes best").
6. Two points in one line. Two independent clauses joined by "and" or a comma, unless they are one parallel fact about two like things ("Teams comes with Microsoft 365, and Google Chat comes with Google Workspace").
7. A trailing clause that only restates or softens ("before the whole company moves", "so people can catch up without reading everything").

PASS when none applies and the sentence gives the bottom line up front: a reader who sees only this line knows the point.

Answer UNKNOWN only when the line is not a full sentence.

Examples a strict editor kept:
- Leaving Slack comes down to hosting, cost and team habits
- Companies on Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace already own a chat tool
- Self-hosted tools put the data and the maintenance on your team
- Topic threads change how a team reads and answers messages
- Discord suits teams that already work with a community there

Examples the same editor struck, with the reason:
- Four questions decide which tool replaces Slack for your team (1: questions do not decide)
- Your reason for leaving Slack sets the shortlist (1: a reason does not set anything; 4)
- An existing software contract often settles the choice before features do (1)
- Zulip groups messages by topic so people can catch up without reading everything (2, 7)
- One team should try the tool before everyone switches (3)
- A move off Slack goes best when you plan it before you pick the tool (2, 4, 5)
- Discord was built for communities, and Campfire runs on a server you host (6, 4)
- A pilot with one team tests the tool before the whole company moves (1, 7)

Judge the form of the sentence only. Do not judge whether its claim is true.
